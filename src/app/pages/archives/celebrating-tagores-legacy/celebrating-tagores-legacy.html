Celebrating Tagore’s legacy, through dance.

NCPA presents three distinct dance styles celebrating the works of Rabindranath Tagore!

Photo credits - Wikipedia

When you talk about Rabindranath Tagore, it is very hard and difficult to miss out on the compositions and the literature that the great man has written. But it is very interesting to see how these literatures are interpreted and shown in different formats. To celebrate his birth anniversary, NCPA had a performance on the literature work of Tagore on 21st May 2026. However, these literatures were presented through the format of dance and that is very interesting to see how unconventional because Tagore did not write these literature with the aim of putting it into a dance format. So when you have such literature, it is very interesting to see how the dancers have adapted that particular content to the dance format, and for that there were three different styles of dance format with Odissi, Kathak and Bharatanatyam.

The first part of the evening started with an Odissi performance by Debamitra Sengupta, who is an internationally acclaimed Odissi dancer whose career has spanned three decades. She is a senior disciple of Guru Kelucharan Mohapatra and Sutapa Talukdar. She has won multiple accolades and is the founder director of the Kolkata Mayur Lalit dance Academy and has contributed to a lot of social outreach programs as a dancer, teacher and a counsellor at the Alipore Correctional Home in Kolkata. She is a top graded Doordarshan artist and is empanelled with the ICCR, the Ministry of External Affairs, the Ministry of Culture, Government of India and Spic Macay. Her acclaimed choreography takes inspiration from Padabali, Kirtan, Sanskrit epics, and Bengali literature with major productions supported by grants from the Ministry of Culture.

She presented a play written by Rabindranath Tagore called Shyama. It is a tale of love, sacrifice and moral conflict, which is based upon a beautiful courtesan called Shyama and a noble foreign merchant called Badraen. The story goes that Badrasen was a noble merchant who came into the region where Shyama was the court dancer and dancing for the king. Badrasen possessed one particular jewel that he had reserved only for his one true love, but the Queen of the region wanted that jewel for herself and sent forward her guards to arrest Badrasen and get that jewel from him. Shyama sees Badrasen get arrested and asks the guards about the issue. She tells the guard to give her two days to show that Badrasen is innocent. Shyama is madly in love with Badrasen. For her, Badrasen is the most handsome man to parade around the Earth. So she knew she had to help him. She then goes to one person, an admirer who has been devoted to her called Uttiyo. She tells him to take the blame upon himself, which ultimately leads to his death. Shyama and Badrasen flee and start to live in a love filled life, but when Badrasen finds out the cost of freedom, he rejects her and Shyama departs heartbroken. The two of them reunite after sometime, but Badrasen notices that Shyama is being honest in her apologies, but he doesn’t want to commit the sin of not forgiving her because he knows she’s being true and honest. This play was beautifully shown with Debamitra playing Shyama and her students acting each role beautifully. The music for this entire production was created in such a way that it was so beautiful to hear, and the melodies were such that they were very sweet sounding. It was a beautiful performance and a very great start to the evening and a wonderful presentation in all.

Following that was a performance by Keka Sinha and her disciples from the Kalapi Centre for Performing Arts. Keka Sinha is a disciple of Bandana Sen and Reba Vidyarthi, both of whom are direct disciples of Shambhu Maharaj from the Lucknow Gharana. She is the recipient of the Singar Mani Award from the Sur Singar Samsad, Mumbai. She is a member of the region selection committee of the Centre of Cultural Resources and Training, Government of India and the East Zone Cultural Centre. She is an ‘A’ graded artist of Doordarshan and is a facilitator and educator of Kathak.

For this performance, a small presentation called Rabi O Raag, which is a bouquet of three songs blended together under the umbrella of Hindustani classical music was presented. This is the first performance where you can see how the Tagore literature has been adapted to different art forms with the literature adapted to Hindustani music for Kathak. The presentation started with a small poem called Pratham Adi Tava Shakti, which is an ode to the primordial energy that keeps the world flowing happily and basking in the light of life. Keka Sinha was joined by two of her students as they delivered a very soothing performance. Following that was another poem, but it focused on the monsoon side of the seasons called Timiro Avagunthane. Here Tagore addresses a mysterious figure who shows up in the courtyard on a dark stormy night, he addresses this being and asks its identity, but the poet only learns that this being is none other than nature. It is nature who creates the monsoon storms and the hustling of trees and the downpour, which reflects the restlessness of the mind. It was a very beautiful performance again by Keka Sinha joined by two more of her disciples, and the last one was a very joyful piece called Anandlokei, which is a celebration of the divine joy and states that the Cosmos is nothing but happiness, beauty and we should all maintain that. This was a very beautiful performance by the ensemble.

Up next was the Bharatanatyam performance by Dr. Chitra Vishwanathan’s students, who is a distinguished exponent of Bharatanatyam with over 26 years of experience in the aspects of performance and pedagogy. She has trained under Geeta Mahadevan and also holds an MFA from the Dr. Sandhya Purecha’s College, Bharata School of Fine Arts. She is known for her landmark Brahma Sanhita choreography, extensive thematic ballets and for training over 2000 students. She also represented India internationally through her work and also did the pioneer work of introducing Manipuri dance in Palghar and in Mumbai. She is also trained in Manipuri, Mohiniyattam, Kuchipudi and Indian folk dance. She is the founder of the Abhinaya Institute of Research and Fine Arts and is the organiser of the Shivanjali classical dance festival since 2014.

For this performance, her students presented two works of Tagore. This begins to the second part of the evening and the last where you can see how Tagore’s compositions were once again adapted to the Carnatic music traditions, which is a very unusual combination. The performance began with Bosanti He Bhuvana Mohini, which is a very vibrant ode to spring with the dancers donning colourful bright costumes and jewellery, and the second one was Pinakite, which is an invocation of Lord Shiva as the cosmic dancer. The two performances were seamlessly blended without any break. It was a very beautiful small performance by the students of Dr. Chitra Vishwanathan.

It was a very beautiful performance and something that was new for me to see the representation and interpretation of Tagore literature into dance format and especially three diverse dances for us, which also shows the love that people have for Tagore and especially dancers. Rabindranath Tagore was a visionary revolutionary writer and was a beacon of hope and symbol for all Bengali people and far more for all Indians and such performances and initiatives really bring out that love that we have for him.
