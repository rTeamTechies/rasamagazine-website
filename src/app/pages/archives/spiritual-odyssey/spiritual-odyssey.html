A Spiritual Odyssey: Dr. Padma Subrahmanyam’s Bhagavad Gita A testimony to time and legacy

On the evening of 22 August 2025, the Experimental Theatre at Mumbai’s National Centre for Performing Arts NCPA transformed into a sacred stage when Padma Vibhushan Dr. Padma Subramanyam presented the Bhagwat Gita, a solo Bharatnrithyam recital that brought back the ancient wisdom to life through dance.

This soulful performance was presented by NCPA, in memory of the late Suvarna Jhaveri Desai, one of the four Jhaveri sisters who were the part of the leading Manipuri troupe today. This performance was part of the August dance residency, 2025, which featured Dr. Subrahmanyam, who will be giving a master class for the following days to students,

The National Centre for the Performing Arts, NCPA, was founded in 1969 by JRD Tata and Dr. Jamshed Baba. It is India’s first multicultural centre dedicated to the preservation and promotion of the performing arts. It is located at Mumbai’s iconic Marine Drive and houses world class theatres like The Jamshed Baba theatre, The experimental theatre, The Tata theatre, The Little theatre and much more. Over the past many decades, the NCPA has become synonymous with excellence hosting international Indian masters across music, dance, drama, and more.

To talk about the leading artist of the evening is never enough. Dr. Padma Subrahmanyam is one of India’s most revered dancers. Born in 1943 into a family that was already deeply rooted in the arts. Her father was a filmmaker and writer, K Subrahmanyam and her mother, Meenakshi was a music composer, thus making Dr. Padma’s early life surrounded by creativity and culture. She trained under Guru Vazhuvoor Ramaiah Pillai in Bharatanatyam and quickly established herself as a performer of immense artistry in Chennai as well as throughout the nation.

However, it was her scholarly curiosity that pushed her forward and put her in a unique place in Indian classical dance. In the 1960s and 1970s, she undertook one of the pioneering research into the sacred book that is the Natya Shastra, which is the ancient Sanskrit treatise on performing arts which is written by the sage Bharat. Her doctoral thesis at Annamalai University reconstructed 108 Karanas, which are the basic dance movements described in the Natya Shastra. These Karanas have been largely absent in Bharatanatyam today due to its difficulty in putting it in the flow of movement, but today she revived those movements through texture study and sculptural references from temple art. For this purpose, she took Shlokas from the Natya Shastra, based them in Carnatic music and then put those Karanas into a movement dance. This has not only enriched the vocabulary of classical dance but has also bridged a millennium of forgotten traditions.

Out of this extensive research done by her was born, Bharatnrityam, her particular and distinct style or Bani as called in Bharatanatyam, which integrated the textual precision of the Natyashastra with the performance framework of Bharatanatyam, including the 108 Karanas.

Today she has received the nation's highest honour, the Padma Bhushan in 1983, The Padma Vibhushan in 2021, the Sangeet Natak Akademi Award and several International recognitions. She is also the founder director of Nrityodaya, in Chennai, a premier institution that trains dancers, musicians and researchers, and has branches in different cities. Dr. Jayashree Rajgopalan is the director and principal at Nrityodaya, Mumbai, who was a part of organising this wonderful event and helping NCPA coordinate with Dr. Padma Subrahmanyam.

Dr. Padma Subrahmanyam was accompanied by a stellar group of musicians all belonging to her family. Dr. Gayathri Kannan was on the nattuvangam and vocal. She is the niece of Dr. Padma Subrahmanyam and has been carrying the legacy laid by her aunt.

On the vocal, Smt. Mahathi Kannan, daughter of Gayathri Kannan and the grand niece of Padma Subrahmanyam, who, with her melodious music as well as range in vocal brought in emotional colour to the musical aspect of the performance.

On the Mridangam was Sri Sarvesh Karthik, who is a young Mridangam artist that has captured and positioned himself as a prominent percussionist in the field

On the Veena, as well as as well as the Shuddha Mandalam was Sri Kannan Balakrishnan, who was also the comparer for the evening. His talent in the Veena as well as the Shuddha Mandalam as well as excellent oratory skills tied in the performance. He is the husband of Dr. Gayathri Kannan as well as the father of Mahathi Kannan. And on the violin was Kumari Sri Lakshmi Bhatt, the youngest of the ensemble. Sri Lakshmi’s violin skills provided lyrical flair to the performance.

Shri Kannan quoted as to why Dr. Padma Subrahmanyam chose to perform the Bhagavad-Gita for this performance, and the reason was that the Bhagwat Gita and Natya Shastra have been identified as intangible heritage by UNESCO and thus it befit the occasion. So, Dr. Padma Subrahmanyam chose to perform the Bhagavad-Gita for her performance and chose to teach Natya Shastra to the students for the three day residency in NCPA.

Dr. Padma Subrahmanyam’s performance started in four acts where she had taken 78 verses from the Bhagavad-Gita and had put them into music to divide the concepts of Bhagwat Gita in an easier format for the audience to understand

The first part talked about Arjuna’s question to Krishna about duty and about peace of mind, which is called Sthitipradnya. The moment Dr. Padma Subrahmanyam stepped foot on the stage, the audience roared with applause as they were witnessing a living legend perform one of her most iconic repertoires.

Dr. Padma Subrahmanyam was adorned in beautiful ornaments and an outfit resembling both Arjuna and Krishna, as she was playing both characters side-by-side. Her beautiful rendition of the verses as well as the surprising jathis performed in the middle of performances, really left the audience shocked.

Act two of the performance focused more on the importance of the Yajnya, and that one should perform their duties without expecting any fruit of their actions. The third act focused on the importance of Prakriti, that is the materialistic word, that is nature, that is the womb where all living beings take birth from. The performance also showcased the three Gunas that are Satthvik, Rajasik and Tamasik and Dr. Padma Subrahmanyam very smartly had involved a jathi with portrayal of the Tamasik Guna, which really left the audience in awe as one couldn't comprehend the artistry and talent of her performance.

The final piece talked about salvation, and where Krishna finally told and showed Arjuna his true form. In the portrayal Arjuna saw his cousins who he was fighting against and his elders being eaten and turned into dust by one of Krishna's many heads. Dr. Padma Subrahmanyam's expressions really brought out this feeling of being scared, terrified, which Arjuna faced while he saw that visual, and in the very end, she showed the portrayal of Vishnu as Kala, he who is here to finish time and is time himself. The finale ended with Arjuna confidently going back into the war

The stunning portrayal of the Bhagavad-Gita by Dr. Padma Subrahmanyam received a standing ovation. At the end of the performance, Darshana Jhaveri, a close friend of Dr. Padma Subrahmanyam, as well as the sister of the late Suvarna Jhaveri came on stage and embraced her friend in a tight hug as well as kissed her cheeks and gave her words of appreciation to the wonderful artistry displayed by Dr. Padma Subrahmanyam and her entire team.

The musicians truly brought out the Bhava, the tense moments, as well as the intricate feelings through their wonderful performance, vocally as well as with the instruments. Their artistry really complimented the overall performance blending with the dance brought out by Dr. Padma Subrahmanyam. The creating a wonderful landscape for the audiences to appreciate.

Dr. Padma Subrahmanyam’s performance was not only a performance, but a sort of way for the audience to reflect and think about these Bhagavad-Gita verses which talk about life, duties, and what one should do to live life authentically, and in a way that you do not eat yourself up.
