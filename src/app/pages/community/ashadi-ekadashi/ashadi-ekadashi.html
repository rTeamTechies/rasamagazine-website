When Ashadi Ekadashi turns Pandharpur into an echo of “Vitthala Vitthala Hari Vitthala”

Video credits – Splash Studios

There is a beautiful tradition that happens every year as the monsoon takes over the country and the state of Maharashtra. Thousands of saffron flags begin to flutter across villages, roads, and highways of Maharashtra with rhythmic melody coming from the Mridang and of the cymbals and the collective chanting of Vitthala Vitthala Jai Hari Vitthala echo In throughout cities, fields, towns. Men, women, children, and the elderly, walking barefoot hundreds of kilometres towards one destination, the temple and the town of Pandharpur.

This beautiful tradition is the annual pilgrimage known as Wari, which is considered amongst India’s oldest and largest living spiritual traditions out of many. This culminates on Ashadi Ekadashi, which is the 11th day of the fortnight of the Shukla Paksha of the Hindu month of Ashada, when millions of devotees gather to worship and seek blessings of their beloved Lord Vitthala, a form of Lord Krishna worshipped in Maharashtra.

The Wari goes beyond a religious procession. It is a celebration of devotion, love, equality, humanity, music, literature, and the most important, community, and this is a tradition that has been prevalent for centuries, carrying forward the beautiful heritage, history and the teachings of Maharashtra’s Sants and poets also continuing to inspire millions in the modern world.

The worship of Vitthala, however, goes beyond the Bhakti Movement, but the tradition started to increase and take shape between the 13th and the 17th century through the inspiration and teachings of Maharashtra’s great Saint poets who showed through their teachings and literature that anyone can have a deeply personal relation with God based on pure love, devotion, compassion, and equality.

The beloved deity of Pandharpur has a unique place in the hearts of many Hindu devotees standing on the brick with his hands on his waist. Vitthala is said to always be eternally waiting for his devotees. The story of this beautiful form of Shri Krishna comes from the devoted devotee Pundalika who dedicated his life to serving his elderly parents. Lord Krishna was so impressed by his devotion, he himself came to visit him, but since Pundalika was occupied, massaging the feet of his parents, he respectfully told Krishna to wait and gave him a brick to stand on outside while he finished serving his parents. Krishna stood upon the brick and waited till Pundalika finished his duty. This particular iconography and the waiting form of Vitthala became a symbol that the idea of service to one’s parents and humanity is itself divine worship.

The Warkari movement became very strong and emotionally resonating because of Saints, whose teachings still continue to shape and teach centuries later. Sant Dnyaneshwar also known as Dnyandev is considered as one of the founding pillars of the Warkari tradition. Sant Dynaneshwar was born into a family emancipated from the society, and from a very early age, he had to experience hardships, but his life turned into a path of spiritual liberation and transformed the world of Marathi literature with his great work, Dynaneshvari, which is a Marathi commentary on the Bhagwad Gita, which made many philosophical ideas accessible to ordinary and common people. Sant Dnyaneshwar was able to open the doors of knowledge to everyone at a time when sacred knowledge was only confined to Sanskrit scholars. He believed that God or divinity resides in every living being, and that devotion is not confined to any caste, birth and social status. His philosophy became the foundation pillar of the Wari movement and the inclusive spirit of the moment. Today the revered palkhi, which carries Sant Dnyaneshwar’s Padukas begins its journey from Alandi to Pandharpur every year. Along with Sant Dnyaneshwar, the palkhis of his siblings Muktabai, Sopandev and Nivruttinath start their journey to Pandharpur.

Sant Namdeev was a companion of Sant Dnyaneshwar and also carried the message of devotion for Lord Vitthala far beyond the state of Maharashtra. He has found a very special place in the Sikh scripture, the Guru Granth Sahib as Sant Namdev and his devotional hymns have travelled across North India. He believed that pure devotion mattered more than any sort of ritual or social hierarchy. His Abhangs are considered as one of the most beloved devotional songs sung during the Wari pilgrimage.

Photo credits - Pinterest

Another special Saint who revived and strengthened the entire Wari tradition during a time while social division was at all time was Sant Eknath. He was a scholar, a poet and most importantly, a reformer who believed in compassion, humility and service. Sant Eknath, through his writing, encouraged all people to respect every individual and treat every other with dignity, irrespective of their social background or occupation. Sant Eknath, through his literary work, focused that true devotion only is reflected when one treats others with love and respect.

Another greatly revered poet and saint who completely transformed the aspect of spirituality into the world of poetry, so that ordinary and common people could understand was Sant Tukaram. Through his numerous abhangs, Sant Tukaram expressed many profound philosophical ideas, truths and beliefs through simple language. He also criticised hypocrisy, greed, caste discrimination, empty rituals which had no meaning and at the same time, he preached honesty, compassion and unwavering devotion to Lord Vitthala. From the city of Dehu, the palkhi which carries Sant Tukaram Maharaj’s paduka starts its journey towards Pandharpur, which draws millions of devotees to walk alongside the auspicious palkhi.

There are many other Saints who enriched this wonderful tradition, including Sant Chokhamela., who was a Dalit Saint who completely challenged the caste tradition and barriers through his unwavering devotion. There was Sant Janabai who used poetry as a medium to express intimate love and devotion for her Lord Vitthala, while reflecting the lives of ordinary women, Sant Savant Mali, who was an ordinary gardener who showed that every day work itself was a form of worship, Sant Gora Kumbhar, who was a humble potter. Sant Narahari Sonar, a Saint who was a goldsmith, Sant Sena Nhavi, Sant Kanopatra, whose life itself became a symbol of spiritual liberation and Sant Bahinabai, who today still inspire many generations.

All these wonderful and spiritual saints represented every aspect and section of society and showed that irrespective of one’s social background, occupation, one can always devote love, respect, devotion, humility to others and that itself is a form of devotion.

The sacred journey of the Wari begins weeks prior to the auspicious day of Ashadi Ekadashi. There are two principal processions that start, one starting from Alandi carrying the padukas of Sant Dnyaneshwar, and the other from the town of Dehu, carrying the padukas of Sant Tukaram Maharaj and along the way, numerous smaller processions from different regions in Maharashtra join the pilgrimage. The devotees then walk approximately 250 km over nearly three weeks with multiple stops a different cities such as Pune, Saswad, Jezuri, Lonand, Phaltan, Malshiras and Akluj and before they enter Pandharpur, they stopped at a small village outside town of Pandharpur called Wakhri, where the palkhi processions finally meet before their final entry into the town of Pandharpur.

There are many auspicious and sacred rituals that are performed during the pilgrimage, such as Ringan. It is considered as a very religious, spiritual and thrilling ritual, wherein the devotees form a huge circle and there is a sacred horse called the Maulincha Ashwa, which runs through the centre while the devotees dance and sing joyous devotional chants of Lord Vitthala. The significance of this tradition is that the devotees try to catch the dust, kicked by the horse who is considered as a holy horse, to touch their forehead. This tradition started around 1832, which blends military style coordination with high spiritual devotion that happens.

The auspicious day of Ashadi Ekadashi is the day where everything culminates together. It is considered as one of the most sacred days in Maharashtra calendar. Various palkhis approach the city of Pandharpur and the town completely turns into a sea of melodious devotional music, community, and millions of devotees gather from every corner of the state and much beyond. The most sacred moment for many occurs when they cross the banks of the auspicious Chandrabhaga river named after a crescent shaped curve. Many pilgrims take a ritual bath in this river before proceeding towards the temple of Lord Vitthala and Goddess Rukmini. On this very auspicious day of Ashadi Ekadashi, there are long queues for the temple which stretch for far kilometres, but the atmosphere is not tense, it is remarkably patient. When I had gone to the town and temple of Pandharpur, we had to wait in line for almost 4 to 6 hours, but it never felt as if we were in a rush. It never felt as if people were getting angry. It never felt as if people were getting intense. Everyone was calm. Everyone was eager, and everyone was waiting for their eyes to fall on the magnificent idol of Lord Vitthala and Goddess Rukmini.

The devotees eagerly wait to stand before Vitthala and touch his feet and gain his manifestation. Lord Vitthala has always been associated as a deity who has an intimate relation with every devotee of his and one unique aspect that I myself found when I had gone to this temple town of Pandharpur that unlike other temples, where you cannot enter the sanctum sanctorum, however, in the temple of Pandharpur of Lord Vitthala, you can go and touch the feet of Lord Vitthala and Goddess Rukmini, which immediately creates such a compassionate bonding that it brings you to tears.

The Wari movement is not just a movement or a tradition that turns into a pilgrimage every year, but it is something that has considerably shaped the cultural identity of Maharashtra. In many ways. It is the literary heritage of Maharashtra. Today the compositions by Sant Dnyaneshwar Sant Namdev, Sant Tukaram and many others are considered as not only poems, but philosophical reflections on life, love, suffering and spirituality. Combining these poems with music devotion creates an atmosphere where faith and love for once divinity becomes a shared musical experience rather than a silent ritual. Even today many schools teach children. These Abhangas ensure that the tradition continues to inspire generations.

The Sants and the literature as well as the traditions show that service to each other that is Seva should always be selfless and that itself is a display of devotion. Today when the pilgrimage happens at numerous spots across the 250 km, there are thousands of organisations and volunteers who organise drinking water, medical assistance, food distribution, sanitation, accommodation, and crowd management for all these pilgrims. As the pilgrims walk into every village, the villagers open their houses and the village with open hearts, providing meals and places to stay free of charge. This itself is considered as a selfless act and an offering to Lord himself, and that’s why the Wari has become a symbol of community.

Today in the changing times of modernity, the Wari tradition still survives. It reminds people to work together rather than run ahead. It reminds people to slow down in life. When things get fast, it serves as a reminder to serve others rather than seeking recognition, it reminds people to sing rather than argue with others. It reminds us to share with others rather than accumulate for one. This journey is not a journey to a temple, but it is a journey for every individual to reflect inward, to forgive, to reconnect and rediscover their own purpose in life.

This Wari tradition is far more than an annual religious event. It is considered as the living cultural soul of Maharashtra, where the world of poetry, philosophy, music, devotional, faith, humanity, all come together. Every Ashadi Ekadashi millions of people and pilgrims finally arrive in the town of Pandharpur. After weeks of walking, they do not complete a physical journey, but they complete a tradition. They believe that God is not found in privilege or in grand celebrations, but he is found in humility, compassion, serving others, unwavering devotion, and equality.

Although the roads leading to Pandharpur may eventually become empty after the celebrations finish, but the spirit of the Wari continues to thrive in the villages, temples, homes, literature, and music of Maharashtra throughout the year.

Today, many generations still walk this wonderful pilgrimage in the footsteps of Sant Dnyaneshwar, Sant Namdev, Sant Tukaram, Sant Chokhamela and many countless others, who have completely transformed the devotional spiritual world of Maharashtra. This pilgrimage is not a mere Pilgrim for Warkaris, it is open to all irrespective of any social bracket of life, and that is the greatest miracle of the Wari tradition that even after 700 years, it’s still continues to bring together millions of people with a message that is still relevant today as it was when the Saints first sang the name of Vitthala.

“Vitthala Vitthala Jai Hari Rukmini Vitthala!”
