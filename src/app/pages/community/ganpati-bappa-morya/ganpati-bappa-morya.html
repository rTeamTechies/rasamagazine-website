Every year, once the monsoons begin to retreat and the month of Bhadrapada arrives, the country of India prepares to welcome one of the most beloved deities. People get to cleaning their houses, mandals begin decorating their pandals, artisans work late into the night, beautiful modaks are prepared and the familiar call of ‘Ganpati Bappa Morya’ begins to fill the streets.

Ganesh Chaturthi, also widely known as Vinayaka Chaturthi, is a festival that celebrates the birth of Lord Ganesha, the elephant-headed deity associated with wisdom, prosperity, good fortune and the removal of obstacles in Hinduism. Traditionally observed on the fourth day of the waxing moon in the Hindu calendar month of Bhadrapada, this festival lasts from the first day and can go up to ten days, with the Visarjan happening on Anant Chaturdashi, that is the tenth day. But this festival is much more than just a religious festival. Over decades, this festival has evolved into a large celebration of more community, art, music, food, identity and particularly in Maharashtra, it's all about public life.

Why is Ganesh Chaturthi celebrated? At its heart, this festival marks the birth of Lord Ganesha. In the Hindu traditions, Lord Ganesha is revered as Vighnaharta, that is the remover of obstacles and as the deity of wisdom and auspicious and new beginnings. It is very important that his blessings are traditionally sought before important ventures, ceremonies or new beginnings. The festival is therefore not just simply about welcoming a deity into one's house; it also represents the hope that the coming year will be full of wisdom, prosperity and the ability to overcome all difficulties. The arrival of Lord Ganesha is celebrated through pran pratishtha, prayers, aartis, offerings and most recognizable offerings in the form of delicious modaks, particularly the steamed ukdiche modak found in Maharashtra. Food, music, devotion are certain aspects that become intertwined during this wonderful festival and it goes from a religious observance into a large social experience. The festival concludes with Visarjan, that is the immersion of the idol in the water. Symbolically, it is believed that Lord Ganesha returns to his divine abode while devotees are reminded of the impermanence of the physical world. The idol is temporary, worshipped and eventually returned to nature.

It is often believed that Lokmanya Bal Gangadhar Tilak started Ganesh Chaturthi, but that is not quite accurate. Ganesh worship and Ganesh Chaturthi celebrations existed long before the 19th century. There are historical accounts pointing to celebrations happening during the Maratha and Peshwa periods, while the festival was traditionally observed within households and communities. But what changed in the late 19th century was the scale and purpose of the celebration. In Pune, Bhau Saheb Laxman Jadhav, associated with the Bhau Saheb Rangari tradition, organized a public Ganesh festival in the year 1892 and in the following year, 1893, Lokmanya Tilak helped transform this wonderful festival into a larger public institution. But why did Lokmanya Tilak make this festival a public affair? The answer lies in colonial media. India at the time was under British rule and political gatherings, nationalist organizations, were heavily restricted. Large political meetings could attract the attention of the government and make it difficult for Indians to gather openly in large numbers. Lokmanya Tilak recognized that religious and cultural gatherings offered a different possibility. By taking part in a festival that had always been observed privately and by encouraging large-scale public celebrations, he created a space where people from different social groups could come together. Ganesh mandals became places for community gatherings, speeches, music, theater and cultural programs. The festival therefore has its political and social dimension as well. Lord Ganesha then became more than just a household deity. He became a symbol around which the communities could gather together. This public form of Ganeshotsav plays a very important role in developing collective consciousness during the freedom movement. Maharashtra Tourism describes Lokmanya Tilak's 1893 transformation of this very festival as a wonderful effort to unite and bring people together during the independence movements and notes that the festival then became associated with a lot of social and cultural unity.

Today, Ganesh Chaturthi is celebrated across large parts of the country, including Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Tamil Nadu, Odisha, West Bengal and many other regions. The rituals, food and style of celebration, however, change from place to place. In Maharashtra, the festival is perhaps the most spectacular. Families bring Ganesha idols into their house, while neighborhood mandals establish public pandals. There are daily aartis, cultural programs, decorations and community activities that continue throughout the festival. In Goa, celebrations often retain a more intimate and family-oriented character. Traditional clay idols are brought into the households and the reflection is brought through the decoration. In Karnataka, Ganesh Chaturthi is closely associated with a lot of regional traditions and often takes place alongside Gauri Habba, dedicated to the Goddess Gauri. The festival combines household worship with a lot of public celebration. In the state of Tamil Nadu, the festival is known as Vinayaka Chaturthi or Pillayar Chaturthi. Lord Ganesha's idols are installed in houses and public spaces, while offerings such as kozhakattai, a preparation related to the modak tradition, are commonly associated with the festival. In states like Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, Vinayaka Chaturthi is celebrated with elaborate idols, pujas and a lot of community gatherings, bringing public installations together and turning it into a part of urban celebration. In West Bengal, where Durga Puja is usually the largest festival, Ganesh Puja also has its own distinctive space. Lord Ganesha is traditionally associated with auspicious beginnings and in recent years, Ganesh celebrations have emerged largely in cities such as Kolkata. These differences across the nation are what makes the festival more fascinating. The God remains the same, but every region gives Ganesh Chaturthi its own language of devotion.

If there is one city where Ganesh Chaturthi seems to overtake everyday life, it is none other than the city of Mumbai. For several days, the city of Mumbai becomes completely different. Roads become procession routes, neighborhoods turn into festival spaces and thousands of devotees move between different pandals, trying to get a look of their favorite god. Mumbai's famous public Ganpatis attract lots of crowds, with elaborate, beautiful idols having all distinctive themes, decorations and aartis. Today, a lot of the idols have become cultural institutions in their own right, drawing visitors from across the city and beyond. But this festival is not only about the biggest idol. When you walk through a housing society, or even a small neighborhood, the experience is just as meaningful. You have small children helping with decorations, families preparing naivedya, residents attending evening aarti and neighbours meeting one another every day. However, the hardest goodbye comes on Visarjan. Mumbai's geography gives the farewell a particularly dramatic character. The procession moves throughout the city towards the sea and at the immersion points, accompanied by drums, dancing and the constant chant of ‘Ganpati Bappa Morya, Pudcha Varshi Laukar Ya’. The immersion becomes both celebration and farewell and an emotional reminder that every arrival eventually leads to a departure.

Ganesh Chaturthi is a festival that has never remained exactly the same. From household worship to public mandals, from a lot of traditional clay idols to elaborate artistic installations, from a regional Maharashtrian celebration to a festival observed across India, the journey of this festival reflects the changing social landscape of India itself. Today, there are many communities who are becoming more conscious of the environmental impact of Visarjan. Traditionally, shadu clay idols, natural colours, smaller idols and artificial immersion pans are increasingly encouraged today as a way of reducing damage to water bodies. Maharashtra tourism has particularly highlighted the use of eco-friendly idols, natural colours, emphasis on water conservation and cleanliness initiatives as part of contemporary celebrations.

However, beneath the lights, music and the grandeur itself, the central idea remains simple and remarkable: a deity arrives and a community comes together. For ten days, homes and streets become spaces of devotion and celebration and then, with music and emotion, Lord Ganesha leaves, only to be welcomed again the following year. This is the enduring power of Ganesh Chaturthi. It is simultaneously ancient and constantly reinvented every year. It is deeply personal and wonderfully public, yet rooted in faith and expressed through art, through music, food and community. From the quiet aarti in a small family home to thousands dancing behind a surging procession in Mumbai, Ganesh Chaturthi has become one of the most recognizable expressions of collective celebrations in India.
