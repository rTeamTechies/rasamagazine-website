Ratha Yatra: A symbol of spirituality and devotion beyond life!

Photo credits - News18

Every year, there is one festival that completely transforms the coastal town of Puri in Odisha and it becomes one of the largest religious gatherings in the world with millions of devotees coming to witness the grand Ratha Yatra of Lord Jagannath and his siblings. The Ratha Yatra is a centuries old festival that sees the three siblings, Jagannath, Balabhadra, and Subhadra emerge from the sanctum of the Jagannath Temple and embark on a journey which is so grand that it fills the streets of Puri with millions of people coming together to witness the beauty and the auspicious divinity that occurs. Ratha Yatra is more than a religious event. It is a celebration of devotion, community, inclusivity and living heritage. At the heart of the traditions, the Jagannath Temple, which is considered Hinduism’s four sacred Char Dham pilgrimage sites. The history of the temple and the Ratha Yatra Festival are deeply woven into the cultural identity of the people of Odisha, which attracts pilgrims, historians, artists, and spiritual seekers across the world to gather for this very spiritual journey.

Jagannath Temple, which stands today as one of the most gorgeous temples in India was built in the 12th century by King Ananthavarman Chodaganga Deva of the Eastern Ganga Dynasty and the temple was further completed by his success. The temple rises majestically above the town of Puri, and has always remained one of India’s most important places of worship for more than a thousand years. But the story of Lord Jagannath starts much earlier and is rooted in a fascinating and mystical blend of history, mythology, and local tribal traditions.

According to a popular legend, King Indradyumna of Malwa was an ardent devotee of Lord Vishnu and he had heard numerous stories of a mysterious deity called Neela Madhava, who was worshipped in secret by a tribal chief name Viswavasu in the deep forest of Odisha. The king was determined to find this divine God and he sent his emissaries across the land but Neela Madhava disappeared, but the king received a divine vision from the Lord himself instructing him to build a grand temple, carve and make new idols from a sacred log of wood that magically washed ashore.

Soon after, a miraculous wood log appeared on the coast, and it is believed that Vishwakarma who said to be, the architect of the Gods arrived disguised as an old craftsman and offered his help to carve the idols on one condition that he wanted to work in complete seclusion, and no one would open the door of the room where he was working until the task was finished.

Many days passed, and there was no sound emerging from the workshop. Everyone grew anxious. Gundicha, the wife of the king, asked her husband to open the door and see for themselves what was going on, and eventually the husband and wife entered the room and the carpenter had vanished. The idols stood their incomplete, lacking fully formed hands and legs. Realising that they had interrupted the divine process, the king was distraught. But a celestial voice reassured the king that the unfinished forms of the three idols were not a mistake, but it is the form that Lord himself wished to be worshipped and thus came the very distinct wooden idol of Lord Jagannath, Lord Balabhadra and Devi Subhadra that are worshipped today.

This particular legend has a special place in the Jagannath tradition as it reflects a very spiritual message. The three deities and the idols are intentionally different from a lot of conventional Hindu iconographies. Their unfinished appearance suggests that sometimes divinity itself transcends physical perfection and human expectations. The idols also reflects the influence of ancient tribal worship practices that have been integrated in the mainstream Hindu tradition. There is a fusion of tribal, Vaishnavite and Shaivaite and even Buddhist influence, which makes the temple and the tradition of Lord Jagannath, one of the most inclusive religious traditions in India and it has always evolved through cultural exchange rather than keeping things rigid.

Over the centuries scenes, like Ramanujaharya, Adi Shankaracharya, Chaitanya, Mahaprabhu, and Guru Nanak visited the city of Puri, contributing to the reputation of the city as a major centre of spiritual learning and devotion. Despite multiple invasions that India has faced as well as political turmoil and social changes. The worship of Lord Jagannath has always endured, making the temple a symbol of resilience as well as continuity.

The Ratha Yatra festival is considered as the most important celebration associated with the temple of Lord Jagannath. This festival, according to multiple historical records has been observed for many centuries and was already renowned throughout India during the mediaeval period.

The festival commemorates the annual journey that Lord Jagannath, his elder brother Balabhadra and younger sister Subhadra take from the main temple to the Gundicha Temple, which is located approximately 3 km away from the Jagannath Temple. The tradition is that Gundicha temple is the home of their aunt, and it is a journey which symbolises a visit to her residence.

There is another legend, which unfolds during the latter half of the Ratha Yatra, which also adds a beautiful and adorable dimension to the divine siblings. Lord Jagannath leaves for Gundicha temple with his brother Balabhadra and young Sister Subhadra, but he leaves behind his wife, Goddess Lakshmi in the Jagannath Temple. It is believed that Goddess Lakshmi gets angry and upset that her husband has departed without her. On the fifth day of the festival, which is known as Hera Panchami, Goddess Laksmi travels in a grand palanquin herself to the Gundicha temple to confront Lord Jagannath and she arrives and Lord Jagannath sends her lots of gifts and a garland promising to return soon but Goddess Lakshmi is still unconvinced. Goddess Lakshmi then expresses her displeasure in a symbolic act by ordering one of her attendants to damage a part of Jagannath’s Chariot, Nandighosha, and then Goddess Lakshmi returns back to the main temple. This ritual is still performed every year during Hera Panchami.

Several days later comes the Bahuda Yatra which is the return journey back to Jagannath Temple. According to Odiya traditions, it is believed the goddess Lakshmi is still upset, and when Lord Jagannath reaches the temple after the procession, she refuses to let him enter and the temple doors remain shut. Lord Jagannath must pacify her with affection, apologies and offerings, and only after this playful reconciliation does Goddess Lakshmi allow him back into the temple. This ritual is still performed today and is remembered as a charming depiction of marital love, separation and reunion, making the Divine couple appear in a very relatable way to the ordinary devotees. It is this reconciliation that completes and marks the end of the emotional cycle of Ratha Yatra.

What distinguishes this festival from other religious festivals is that the deities actually leave the sanctum and come amongst people, and this has always been significant because not everyone can enter the temple. Ratha Yatra then becomes an opportunity for all devotees, regardless of any background, to receive the blessing of Lord Jagannath. This festival is often described, hence as the occasion when God himself comes out of his temple to meet humans, rather than waiting for humanity and humans to come to God.

Ratha Yatra is observed on the second day of the fortnight of the Hindu month of Ashada, which usually falls in June or July. Before the Ratha Yatra, there is another occasion that falls, which is a ceremonial bathing festival in which the three siblings are bathed with 108 pots of sacred water and following this ritual, it is believed that the three gods fall ill and they remain secluded from the public view during a period known as Anasara.

These few days of being away from the Lord makes devote feel that sense of longing and thus, Ratha Yatra marks the joyous moment when the three gods reappear and begin their journey through the city of Puri and spiritually, this journey represents divine accessibility, compassion, and the breaking down of barriers between the sacred and the ordinary world.

The most significant aspect of the Ratha Yatra is the construction of three giant chariots which happens every year. Unlike many other religious processions of other temples, they use ceremonial vehicles which can be reused, but for the procession of Lord Jagannath and his siblings, chariots are built every year using traditional methods which have been passed down through generations.

The three chariots have three names. Lord Jagannath travels in the chariot called Nandighosha, Balbhadra travels in the chariot called Taladhwaja and Subhadra travels in the middle chariot called Darpadalana. Hundred to almost thousand artisans take part in constructing and making these wooden structures which are towering across the city of Puri while still preserving techniques that are centuries old. The chariot themselves are a symbol of moving masterpiece and sacred craftsmanship.

Another powerful factor of Ratha Yatra is that thousands and millions of devotees come forward to pull the giant chariot. Participation is considered as an act of immense spiritual merit.

Before the procession begins, the Gajapati king of Puri performs the ritual of Chhera Pahanra which is basically sweeping the chariots with a golden broom, and it shows that a king can also become a servant before the Lord. The ritual reinforces the idea that everyone is equal in the eyes of the Lord.

However, there is one particular aspect of this entire the entirety of Lord Jagannath and Jagannath Temple that cannot be complete without mentioning the ardent devotee Salabega who was a celebrated Muslim devotee whose story serves as a symbol of religious harmony. Salabega was born to a Moghul father and Hindu mother in the 17th century and had deep devotion towards Lord Jagannath and composed numerous devotional songs in Odia.

Out of all the songs that he has composed ‘Ahe Nila Saila’, that is ‘A Blue Mountain’, which is a heartful prayer, which seeks the Lord’s grace, and compassion and remains as the most beloved poems in the hearts of all Odia people.

There are many legends associated with the story ‘Ahe Nila Saila’, one such story says that Salabega once traveled to Vrindavan and while returning to Puri, he realised he would not be able to arrive in time for Ratha Yatra and he was heartbroken at that thought he didn’t want to miss Lord Jagannath’s darshan for an entire year, and he composed an earnest prayer, asking the Lord to wait for him and according to legend, it is believed that during the yatra at that time, Lord Jagannath’s chariot, which is Nandighosha reached the Grand Road near Balagandi, but it stopped moving. Thousands of devotees came to pull the ropes. Elephants were brought in, but the chariot would not move at all. It only started to move once Salabega finally arrived before his beloved Lord and offered his prayers. Every year, even today, Lord Jagannath’s chariot pauses and stops briefly near Salabega’s Samadhi on the Bada Danda, Grand Road. This is the festival's most moving tradition, which believes not only in miracles, but also in the belief of a man who had sincere devotion, transcending all religion, caste and social identity.

Ratha Yatra of Puri is much more than just a procession. It is a symbol of living traditions, which have been carried out for centuries of history, mythology, craftsmanship, and philosophy. From the legend of the unfinished wooden to the annual journey that all the three siblings take throughout the streets of the city of Puri shows and speaks of accessibility, inclusiveness and love which is divine. The story of Salabega and the popularity of ‘Ahe Nila Saila’ reinforces these wonderful values and reminds us that Lord Jagannath is not a regional deity or a temple, God, but is a universal presence whose embrace extends beyond all human divisions.
