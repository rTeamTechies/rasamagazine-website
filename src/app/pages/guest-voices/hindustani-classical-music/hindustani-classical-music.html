The Rich Tapestry of Hindustani Classical Music: An Explainer for Noobs

By Dhruva Upadhye

(Pa Ni Ni Sa)*4, Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni Dha Pa Dha Ni Dha Pa Dha Ni Sa! Ever wondered where does this intricate pattern of notes in ‘Mere Dholna’ or even ‘Ghar More Pardesiya’ come from? They are the appropriation of Hindustani Classical Music, typeshit, or rather, one of the many ways in it manifests in pop culture. Unfortunately, very little effort is made to actually understand this system of music, hence this explainer article!

The origins of Classical Music in the Indian subcontinent can be traced back to the Vedic times, especially during the composition of ‘Samaveda’ around 1200 to 1000 BCE. The Samaveda made references to Richas/Ruchas i.e. the verses of the Rigvedas and taught us how to chant or sing these verses rhythmically. Over centuries, this music evolved and became much more complicated- with greater number of rules and conventions dictating it. Ancient Indian Classical Music consisted of Saamagayan (group/choir singing) and also Prabandha/jaatigayan (singing of jaatis), etc. It is believed that for the longest time until the medieval ages, India only had a single school of classical music. Eventually, foreign conquests and rule in the North such as that of the Mughals and other dynasties (eg. Ghaznis) influenced the music landscape of the subcontinent. Indian classical music underwent a major shift- the music in the north, which is today known as Hindustani Classical Music emerged as a separate branch of music because of the foreign influence. This amalgamation of the local culture with the foreign is not only limited to music but can also be seen many other aspects of our lifestyle like art, architecture, food, clothing, language, dance, etc. No cap, the music in South India, being negligibly exposed to foreign culture, retained a lot of its original character. Today, this branch is called as the Carnatic or the South Indian Classical Music. What logically follows is that Carnatic Music might be more similar to the Classical Music of Ancient times in comparison to Hindustani Music.

Now, it is understood that in the present day, Hindustani and Carnatic music are two branches of the Ancient Classical Music, geographically associated with the North and the South of the country respectively. Given that they have a common ancestor, the fundamentals or the building blocks of both lowkey remain the same. Two most important elements of both these styles are ‘Swar’ and ‘Taal’. Swaras refer to the ‘sur’ or the melodious sounds; Sa Re Ga Ma Pa Dha Ni together constitute the 7 swaras. Swaras can be played on different kinds of instruments like the Sitar, Veena, Violin, Harmonium, Flute, etc. Most importantly however, is the Tanpura- a string instrument used to create a continuous, resonant harmonic drone, and provide a steady sur reference. Taal on the other hand refers to a combination of fixed beats which is played on loop, in a cyclic manner. For example, Teentaal consists of 16 beats numbered from 1 to 16. After the 16th, the 1st beat is played again and the cycle continues. Percussion instruments like the Tabla, Mridangam, Ghatam, Pakhavaj, etc are used to show the taal. Naturally, a song is composed out of swaras and is set to a fixed taal.

No shit, swar are Taal are intrinsic to all forms of Music around the world whether it is classical, bollybood, folk music, EDM, or even k-pop. However, the most defining feature of Indian Classical Music (including both its styles) is the concept of ‘Raag’. ‘Modes’ in western classical music are the closest equivalent to a Raag, but do not fully capture its notion. Imagine you are asked to form a 5-to-7-digit number which must consist of digits from 1 to 7. One can come up with the numbers like 12345, 21345, 345127, 134567, 7654321, etc. By using numerous permutations and combinations, many numbers can be formed. If we replace the digits 1 to 7 with the 7 swaras mentioned earlier, then the resulting numbers would become ‘Raags’. In other words, Raag is a specific permutation and combination of swaras used to create melodies. For ease of reference, associate each number from 1 to 7 with the respective swar, Sa being 1 and Ni being 7. Raag Bhoop (or Mohanam as it is called in Carnatic Music) consists of only 5 swaras- Sa Re Ga Pa Dha (12356). Hence, all compositions or songs prepared in Raag Bhoop will only consist of these swaras and would not include Ma and Ni (4 and 7) at all. So, a combination like Dha Pa Ga Pa Ga Re Sa (6535321) is also Bhoop because it omits Ma and Ni (4,7). By merely using these 5 swaras myriad patterns can be formed- Pa Dha Pa Pa Sa Sa Dha Pa Ga Re Sa (56551165321), etc. On the other hand, if we use a Ni (7) instead of Dha (6), the resulting combination will be called Raag Hamsadhwani. So Hamsadhwani goes like- Sa Re Ga Pa Ni (12357). As a result, Ni Pa Ga Pa Ga Re Sa Ni Sa Re Ga Pa Ga Re Sa (753532171235321) is Hamsadhwani as long as Ma and Dha (4,6) are omitted.

Sheesh, this is barely scratching the surface of the plethora of rules that shape a Raag. For example- every raag must have Sa (1). Secondly, instead of 7 swaras, Indian Classical Music consist of 12! Yes, you heard that right. What the remaining five called? Va, Ne, Yo, Sup? No, they don’t have distinct names. This is because the swaras Re, Ga, Ma, Dha and Ni have 2 versions each- the normal ones are called ‘Shuddha swaras’ and the beta ones are called ‘Vikrut swaras’. Think of it as Re Shuddha being 2 and Re Vikrut being 1.5. Likewise, you’ll have 1.5, 2.5, 4.5, 5.5 and 6.5 for the beta/vikrut versions of Re Ga Ma Dha Ni (2,3,4,6,7)…[can I say 6 7 again?] With a total of 12 swars now from 1 to 7 (including the decimal digits), many more Raags can be created. Eg. (1,1.5, 2.5, 4, 5, 5.5, 6.5) is Raag Bhairavi. Maybe Maths wasn’t the best analogy to explain Raags, lil cooked.

What every raag also has is a defined Main Swar or the Main Lead (Vaadi Swar), Second Most Important Swar or the Second Lead (Samvaadi Swar), a designated (preferable) time of singing it and also the mood or the emotions it evokes (aka Rasa, the magazine’s name). Let’s take Bhoop: Vaadi Swar is Ga while Samvaadi is Dha, it should be sung between 6 pm to 9 pm and has a Bhakti (devotion), Shanta (peaceful) Rasa.

Moving on the another very crucial aspect of Hindustani Music is that of ‘Khayal’. Khayal or Khyal (from the song Kabhi Kabhi Mera Dil Mein Khayal Aata Hai Lol) is a Persian word which literally translates to Thought or Imagination. In music though, it refers to a ‘song’ (which is also called a ‘Bandish’ as used in Bandish Bandits). All songs/khayals in classical music are necessarily composed in a particular Raag. Hence, the khayal becomes a medium or tool to express the Raag. Earlier, during the medieval times, Hindustani Music was dominated by ‘Drupad Gayaki’, however, Muhammad Shah 'Rangila’, a Mughal Emperor introduced Khayal Gayaki for the first time at the Mughal court in the 18th Century. One of his musicians, Niyamat Khan (aka Sadarang) composed khayals and popularized them. Since then, Khayal has been a mogger, replacing Drupad to be the most mainstream or popular mode of presentation in Hindustani Music. Depending on the speed/tempo (‘Laya’), a Khayal can either be a Bada Khyal (a slower and more elaborate presentation) or a Chota Khyal (as the name suggests a medium or fast-paced presentation).

You might wonder, why was the word Khayal used in Hindustani Classical Music? Skibidi, it’s a deliberate choice. While singing, it is expected that an artist expresses his own creativity or imagination through the khayal/song. It is because of khayal that Hindustani Classical Music offers a lot more artistic freedom as compared to Carnatic Music, which is relatively more constrained or stricter. Nonetheless, an artist is required to be mindful of the raag rules even while they are singing the khayal/song. Various techniques such as aalaps, bolaalaps, taans, etc are used in khyal gayaki to fully explore the raag and entertain the audience. While aalaps are slower and used to establish the identity of a raag, taans are fast-paced, intricate melodies adding excitement and thrill to the performance. These techniques of ‘improvisation’ or ‘Raag Vistaar’ are thought by the artist at the moment, which means that an artist creates these aalaps and taans on the spot like an extempore speech. Some may call it divine intervention, but this can only be possible with years of training (riyaaz/sadhana). To summarize, a khayal is a song composed in a specific raag, which showcases artistic freedom, talent, prowess and rizz.

It is interesting how Hindustani Music has also shaped and paved way for semi-classical music in North India. These musical forms may sound like Classical Music, and in many cases may also be composed in a raag, but they differ in their content. Unlike Khyal, Semi-classical music includes Ghazals, Qawwalis, Thumris and Tappas as well as regional variants like Rabindra Sangeet in Bengal and NatyaSangeet in Maharashtra. Though it can be said that semi-classical music is somewhat raag-based, it may not strictly conform to the rules of a raag and the artist is allowed to cook beyond the raag’s limits. Additionally, a greater emphasis is put on the lyrics or the poetry of these compositions rather than solely focusing on the musicality of it.

Today, not only the content, but also the pedagogy, presentation and promotion of Hindustani Music has evolved. There is a gradual realization to undo the gatekeeping of Classical Music by the privileged sections and to make it more accessible. The method of passing down knowledge through oral tradition, as seen in Guru-Shishya Parampara, remains the primary mode of learning. Such learning essentially involves mimicking and perfecting what the Guru teaches before applying one’s own artistic mind to it. Furthermore, the system of Gharanas within Hindustani Music (a distinct lineage or school within Hindustani music, eg. Jaipur, Agra, Kirana, etc) is slowly disappearing due to the standardization and modernization of music. The process of standardization can be attributed to having textbooks of music, as well as written and practical exams bound by a fixed syllabus. These exams provide certification and accreditation to music students similar to mainstream education. Likewise, the modernization of classical music through historical and sociological research and formal studies, increased exposure in the form of concerts, workshops, YouTube channels, Spotify playlists, podcasts and news interviews, have broadened the horizon. Additionally, technological developments have made it possible to even have a tanpura in an app or an electronic tabla (a speaker-like device), without actually requiring instrumentalists or physical instruments for daily riyaaz.

On the contrary, the need to preserve and popularize classical music is felt more today than ever before. This is because of the massive demand for and consumption of mainstream music. In this globalized world, even Anime OSTs jazz music pose or even ASMR idk ig, act as a threat to the survival of classical music. Hence, there is a constant need to make the masses aware about this musical heritage. Collaborations and fusion with western or cinema music is one of the ways to introduce classical music to newer audiences. Another way is to develop a liking step-by-step, one can first try listening to old Bollywood songs, then move to the aforementioned forms of semi-classical music like Ghazals and Qawwalis and finally listen to Classical Music. As a student of this living art form, this article was my humble attempt to acquaint you to its rich tapestry and if you’ve managed to read it till the end, you’re no less than an alpha!
