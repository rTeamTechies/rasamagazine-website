Portuguese Church: Where Faith Meets Modern Architecture

Modern architecture blending in tradition and symbolism

By Ethan Fortes

Within the hustle and bustle of the busy Dadar streets stands an iconic yet controversial church, Our Lady of Salvation also known as Portuguese Church. On a rather chaotic Saturday rush, I joined a few youth on an architectural visit to this particular church as part of a monthly program called heART to heART. This monthly meet-up among the Catholic youth of Bombay has them visiting a new Church every month exploring the art and architecture within and understanding the deeper significance these structures hold.

The Church and Parish of Our Lady of Salvation has a history extending over 400 years. It is one of the oldest churches in Mumbai. The old church was originally built by Portuguese Franciscans in the late 1500s, located in what was then known as lower Mahim and now Dadar. The Portuguese Franciscans carried on as Vicars of Salvacao until 1720 when they had to quit Mahim and Mumbai. In 1851, it reverted to Padroado when the Vicar of the Church, Fr Braz Fernandes offered his priestly services and the church to the Padroado Vicar General.

The older structure of the Portuguese Church in Dadar was very gothic in nature with tall spires and pointy towers. However, in 1974, a decision was made by Cardinal Valerian Gracias and Msgr. F.X. Vaz that a new structure be built for the Church. Cardinal Gracias wanted the Church to be a reflection of the recently concluded Vatican II council. Charles Correa, India’s leading architect, transformed Our Lady of Salvation Church, into a radical departure from the Gothic design so common among many of the other churches across the city. He introduced soaring concrete shells, natural light, and open, community-oriented spaces. This was a reflection of Correa’s architectural philosophy. Now known as the Father of Modern Architecture, he preferred to blend modern sensibilities and Indian climate needs into his architectural designs.

Correa’s idea of architecture was always focused on designing structures that fit the climatic conditions of the place. His deviation from the traditional gothic structure and high glass ceilings of the Church was due to the fact that that was purely European style of architecture that worked towards trapping heat within in generally cooler climates. However, in a city like Mumbai, where there is already a considerable amount of heat and humidity, Correa believed that open spaces helped bring in cross-ventilation helping in bringing the cooler breeze for the people within.

The iconic conical structures of Our Lady of Salvation Church Dadar is also part of Correa’s understanding of cooling an area through breeze. The hot air which is considerably lighter than cooler air tends to rise on top. The conical structures work as a way of guiding the hot air upwards and letting the cooler air settle down. Within the Church itself, there are 4 conical structures, reflecting 4 stages of Jesus’ life: Baptism, Ministry, Crucifixion and post-Resurrection. Each of these conical structures house within itself parts of the Church that reflect the paintings atop the cones. Like, for example, the cone that resembles the Baptism has the baptistry placed there. The Baptistry is the common place where the Sacrament of Baptism takes place. It is said that the design of the building by Correa is so well-done that if all the doors of the Church were open, there would be a constant flow of air by way of the cross-ventilation technique he envisioned.

Within the Church, at first glance, one would feel like Correa would have probably cheated the Church authorities with unfinished interiors. This is clearly visible through the visible stone creases, the cement overlays and the spokes used to hold the cement structure in place as it was being built. However, this is also part of Correa’s design philosophy. In all his work he preferred to leave the structure as is, showing its originality and not covering or beautifying it by plastering the rough edges. Further, he also wanted to show that a Church structure isn’t our permanent dwelling and that our final dwelling place is in Heaven with God. To do this the cones that also resemble a tent like structure have this seemingly unfinished work within to show temporariness on earth as we are all pilgrims here on Earth.

Complementing the unique design of Charles Correa, the concrete domes or shells have a glass fresco on their skylight. This is the creation of renowned Indian artist, M.F. Husain, who is known for his bold and vibrant paintings. The stained skylight, although aged, remains a prominent feature of these domes. With each dome reflecting the different stages of Jesus’ life, Husain has depicted the different stages by way of paintings. A particularly notable example is the skylight of the main altar dome that has got multiple portrayals of Jesus’ ministry like the multiplication of loaves and the raising from the dead among a few.

However, due to its striking deviation from the traditional archaic structures of Churches seen elsewhere, this design of the Church has been widely criticized by laity across the city. The beauty and planning of this modern structure is hidden to many and there is a need to make this known among the people of Mumbai.
